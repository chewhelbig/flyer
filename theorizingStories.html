What I have adopted from Norman Denzin

Norman K. Denzin (1941–2023)

American sociologist, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. Co-editor of the Sage Handbook of Qualitative Research. Founder of the International Congress of Qualitative Inquiry. Author of Interpretive Autoethnography (2013).

Facts and facticities
Facts are what happened. Facticities are how those facts were lived and experienced. Therapeutic autoethnography writes the facticities.
Truthful fiction
A story is crafted, and it stays faithful to facts and facticities. It “creates verisimilitude, or what are for the reader believable experiences” (Denzin, 2013, p. 29).
The epiphany
The story turns on a moment that leaves its mark on the people in it. In the therapy room, even the very first contact is data-rich.
What a story needs
Living characters drawn from real persons · the aesthetics of the setting · an epiphany or crisis · a temporal order true to the case · a meaning that arises from reading it.
The writer inside the text
The researcher’s own lived experience is the material. The therapist writes from within the field she is part of.
11:28 · The facticities
Facts: what happened. Facticities: how it was lived.After Norman Denzin, American sociologist

Now we write the facticities.

Go back to the first minutes of a session
  • What is there?
  • What is it like in you, and in the room, as the client walks in and takes their seat?
  • On Zoom: where is the client? What is behind them? Your first impression of the screen?
  • What is your body doing as the client comes in?
  • What is the air like between you?
  • The first words. The tone of your voices.
  • What do you hear, see, feel, smell, taste?
  • What do you notice now that you did not notice then?
Before you write

Show more, tell less

Nothing is invented. You are reporting the phenomenon, as closely as you can.

  • Leave out the adverbs. Not slowly, but what her steps are.
  • Leave out named emotions, unless someone says them aloud.
  • Leave out interpretation, unless it is a thought you had.
The facticities

Write it from inside

  • First person, present tense
  • Focus purely on the first minutes of contact with the client
  • What you see, hear, and feel in your body. The atmosphere of the room.
  • Leave out diagnosis and formulation
  • Not more than 300 words

Write before you know what you think.

12:35 · A story of mine

Tina attended her first session on 5 October 2017. She arrived ten minutes late.

She brought a cardboard folder of medical records, psychiatric assessments and proof of residency, and offered to send soft copies. Each page of the assessments was stamped. They recorded a diagnosis of obsessive-compulsive disorder, made by a psychiatrist when she was sixteen, after a breakdown during her school examinations. Later reports added panic disorder, migraine and an autoimmune condition. She had been treated with exposure and response prevention some years earlier and had stopped. She took no medication. Her parents were against it.

Tina was born in 1997 in Asia. She completed her degree at a university there and came to Vienna in the summer of 2017 on a research visa, to a junior position at the research and development facility of a technology company. The salary did not cover the cost of living in the city. She planned to apply to graduate school in the United States.

She lived alone. She spoke to her parents every day. They did not know she was in therapy.

She complained of stomach pains, headaches and poor sleep, and of anxiety, probably related to difficulty adjusting to life in Vienna. She described anxiety at work, where she was juggling several projects and felt guilty when a task was left undone. Her checking had intensified since she arrived. Before any new activity, including going to the supermarket, she searched for it online. She said, “I Google everything.”

We agreed to meet weekly.

12:51 · Closing words

Therapeutic autoethnography: the skeleton (Chew-Helbig, 2023)

Premise
The person of the therapist shapes outcome (Wampold & Brown, 2005). So we study who the therapist is while in session. The therapist is the research instrument.
Field
The therapeutic encounter, everyone in it. It begins before the first appointment.
Material
Session notes, intake forms, messages, artwork, and the therapist’s own recollection.
Erlebnis
Lived experience is pre-reflexive and belongs to the aesthetic realm (Gadamer, 1960/1975). Writing is the inquiry (Richardson & St. Pierre, 2018).
Two voices
Scenes in the first person, present tense. Commentary in the third person: insight, hindsight, theory, critique. A polyphony.
Show, do not tell
Stay with the senses. Leave out named emotion and analysis. Give the reader room to enter the field (van Manen, 2016).
Theorizing stories
Theory and story exist symbiotically, frequently told together.
Worthiness
Substantive contribution · grounded scientific perspective · aesthetic merit · reflexivity · impact on writer and reader.
Ethics
Mask identities; composites help, and are not enough. Consent alone cannot answer the power differential. Treat everyone as vulnerable, the researcher included. Publish nothing those written about would not wish to read.
What makes Gestalt therapy Gestalt?

The Swiss PAP-S study: raters, blind to the therapist’s school, coded every intervention in audio-taped sessions across ten modalities.

51–73%of interventions were nonspecific, in every school
5–33%were specific to the therapist’s own approach

No correlation was found between adherence to a school’s techniques and outcome.

The more experienced the therapist, the fewer school-specific interventions: over 30% for less experienced therapists, 10–30% for the more experienced.

Schulthess, P., Crameri, A., Koemeda-Lutz, M., Tschuschke, V., & von Wyl, A. (2021). British Gestalt Journal, 30(1), 19–30. · Schulthess, P., Tschuschke, V., Koemeda-Lutz, M., von Wyl, A., & Crameri, A. The elephant in the room

By Nicole Chew-Helbig. Written in Dubrovnik on the last morning of the EAGT Conference, and presented at the end-of-conference reflection panel, 25 October 2026.

Listen · 2 min 21 s

I woke at three again this morning. I realise that my body is still in Singapore.

And for the last three days, all I wanted was to be here. I knew I would meet people who are special to me, people I only ever see at Gestalt events. If I hadn’t bumped into you this time, I might never have seen you again. I’m grateful I was encouraged to come. I met you, I’m still meeting you, and I’ve met many more of you who will make me want to come back, in the hope of bumping into you again.

So I asked myself: when I fly home this evening, what will be different? Who do I still need to talk to before I leave?

The elephant in the room.

This morning, before breakfast, I put the elephant on an empty chair.

And then I sat in it.

This is what came.

A felt reality. Despair. Darkness. Isolation. Fear. A lot of fear.

I asked: what is this fear about?

The fear of never being met. And the knowledge that being fully met is impossible.

A horrible thought.

I think this is our true suffering.

And the elephant said to me: I’m lonely.

Tell your friends to keep coming back together, and to keep doing this.

And thank those who chose not to come. Their absence was also a way of making contact.

The more we try to make contact, the clearer I become.

This is what makes Gestalt therapy what it is.

The courage to keep being shaped, and to suffer it.

And the elephant said:

Ich werde gestaltet.I am being shaped.Wir werden gestaltet.We are being shaped.Wir sind Gestalt.We are Gestalt.
